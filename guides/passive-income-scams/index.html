Passive income scams: how to spot them before they cost you

By the Lidquid team · Updated October 16, 2026 · 4 min read

the short answer

Most passive income scams follow one pattern: they promise easy money, then get you to pay or move money first. That might be a course, a "starter fee", a cheque you have to partly send back, or a deposit you can't withdraw. Legitimate income only flows to you.

  • Never pay to unlock earnings.
  • Never forward money from a payment you received.
  • Never invest because a stranger online told you to.

Why are passive income seekers such a big target?

Because the promise of money for little effort is exactly what scammers sell. People searching for passive income are often short on time or cash, which makes "it runs itself" and "guaranteed returns" persuasive. Scams in this space rarely look like scams. They look like opportunities, with slick websites, testimonials and countdown timers.

The good news is that the scripts repeat. Learn the main patterns below and you'll recognize most of them on sight.

What are the most common passive income scams?

1. Guru courses and endless upsells

The pitch: someone made a fortune with dropshipping, trading or "AI side hustles" and will show you how for a fee. Then comes the coaching tier, the mastermind and the "inner circle". Not every course is a scam, but if the seller's main income is selling the course, the method is the product. Be especially wary of income screenshots, pressure tactics and refund policies that are hard to find.

2. "Done-for-you" stores and businesses

You pay for a pre-built online store, app, YouTube channel or "automated" Amazon business that supposedly earns while you sleep. In practice these are often hard to exit and fail to deliver what was promised, and the ongoing fees fall on you.

3. MLM and recruitment-based schemes

Multi-level marketing sells "be your own boss" and "residual income". You buy inventory or a starter kit, then earn mainly by recruiting others who do the same. If the money depends more on recruiting than on selling to real customers, walk away.

4. Fake cheques and overpayment (including car wrap scams)

You're offered money to wrap your car in an ad, act as a "mystery shopper" or advertise on your belongings. A cheque arrives for more than expected, and you're asked to deposit it and send the extra to an "installer" or "agent". The cheque later bounces, and the money you sent is gone. The FTC and other consumer agencies have warned about car wrap scams for years. The same script now gets adapted to laptops, phones and clothes. See is laptop sticker advertising legit?

5. Task and "liking" scams

A message offers pay for simple online tasks: liking videos, rating products, "optimizing" apps. The first small payments arrive to build trust. Then you're told to deposit your own money to unlock bigger tasks or withdraw earnings. That deposit is the scam.

6. Crypto and "investment" scams

Fake trading platforms, "guaranteed" returns and investment tips from new online friends or romantic contacts are a major source of losses. One common version builds a friendship or relationship over weeks before steering you toward a "platform" where your balance appears to grow. When you try to withdraw, there are fees, taxes or excuses, and the money is gone. No legitimate investment guarantees high returns with no risk. This is general information, not financial advice.

7. Rental listing scams

If you rent out space or gear for passive income, watch for "renters" who overpay and ask for a refund of the difference, insist on paying outside the platform, or ask you to click a link to "verify" your account. Keep payments inside the platform you listed on, and verify renters through the platform before handing over keys or equipment. If a renter's story keeps changing or they push to skip the platform's protections, cancel the booking.

What are the red flags to watch for?

Any one of these is reason to stop and check. Two or more, and you should walk away.

  • You pay first: fees, kits, deposits, "unlock" or "verification" charges
  • Money moves through you: deposit this, send that on
  • Guarantees: fixed income, no risk, "can't lose"
  • Urgency: limited spots, countdown timers, "decide today"
  • Unusual payment methods: gift cards, wire transfers, crypto, payment apps to a stranger
  • Recruitment: you earn by bringing in friends
  • Vagueness: no clear company, address or explanation of where the money comes from
  • Unsolicited contact: a message out of nowhere offering easy money

How do you check whether an opportunity is legit?

Ask where the money comes from. A legitimate passive income stream can explain, in one sentence, who pays and why. Rental income comes from renters, cashback from retailers paying for referrals, and Lidquid earnings from brands paying for verified scans of a sticker on your laptop.

Then check:

  1. Search the company name plus "scam", "reviews" and "complaints".
  2. Check that a real business stands behind it, with contact details and clear terms.
  3. Read how and when you get paid, and whether money ever has to leave your account.
  4. Talk it over with someone you trust before committing. Scams rely on isolation and speed.

What should you do if you've been targeted?

Stop engaging and stop sending money, even if you're told one more payment will release your funds. Then:

  • Contact your bank or card issuer straight away if you sent money or shared card details. Ask about reversing payments and securing your account.
  • Change passwords and turn on two-factor authentication if you shared logins or installed anything.
  • Report it. In the US, report to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Elsewhere, contact your national consumer protection or fraud reporting agency.
  • Beware recovery scams. People who contact you promising to get your money back for a fee are often the same scammers again.
  • Keep records: screenshots, messages, transaction IDs and the names used.

What do safer passive income options look like?

They're boring, explainable and never ask you to pay to get paid. Think cashback on spending you'd do anyway, renting out unused space through established platforms, interest on savings you already have, or a sponsored laptop sticker if you work in public. Questions people ask

What is the biggest sign of a passive income scam?
Being asked to pay or move money before you're paid, whether that's a fee, a deposit, a starter kit or sending back part of a cheque.
Are passive income courses scams?
Not all of them, but be cautious. If the seller earns mainly by selling the course, uses income screenshots and pressure tactics, or makes refunds hard, treat it as a red flag.
How does the car wrap scam work?
You're offered money to advertise on your car, receive a cheque for more than expected, and are asked to send the extra to an installer. The cheque bounces and the money you sent is lost.
Where do I report a passive income scam?
In the US, report it to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and contact your bank. In other countries, use your national consumer protection or fraud reporting agency.
Can I get my money back after a scam?
Sometimes, if you contact your bank or card issuer quickly. Be wary of anyone offering to recover your money for a fee, because that's often a second scam.
