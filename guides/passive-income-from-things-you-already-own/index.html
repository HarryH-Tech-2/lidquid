What counts as passive income from your own stuff?

It's money an asset you already have earns for you, with little ongoing effort after a one-time setup. The test is simple: once it's listed, does the money keep coming if you mostly ignore it?

Very little income is completely passive. Almost everything needs a listing, a few messages, some upkeep or occasional problem-solving. The honest version of the question is how much work per dollar, and that's how this guide labels each idea:

  • Truly passive: set it up once and it runs, with rare check-ins.
  • Mostly passive: regular but light admin, such as handovers, messages and cleaning.
  • Not passive: it earns, but it's really a small job.

Earnings for all of these vary widely by city, demand, season and how well you set things up. That's why you won't find dollar promises here.

How do you rent out a parking spot or driveway?

You list it on a parking app or local listings site, set the hours and price, and share access instructions with drivers. Once it's set up there's very little to do, which makes it one of the most genuinely passive options.

It works best near offices, stations, stadiums, hospitals and city centers, where parking is scarce. Before listing, check:

  • your lease or HOA rules, since many don't allow subletting a space
  • whether the spot is actually yours to rent (some are assigned, not owned)
  • how drivers get in without you being there

Can you rent out storage space in your home?

Yes. Peer-to-peer storage marketplaces such as Neighbor match people who need space with people who have an empty garage, basement, shed or closet. It's mostly passive, though you'll handle occasional access requests.

Set clear rules about what can be stored (no hazardous materials, no perishables) and when renters can access it. Check what your home insurance covers for other people's belongings, and read the platform's protection terms before agreeing to anything.

Is renting out your car or gear worth it?

It can be if the item sits unused most of the time, but it's mostly passive rather than truly passive. Handovers, cleaning, condition checks and the occasional dispute all take time.

Peer-to-peer car sharing platforms like Turo let you rent out a car you rarely drive. Cameras, drones, tools, camping and sports gear can be rented through gear-sharing apps or local groups. The trade-off is wear and depreciation: every rental puts miles or hours on something you'll eventually want to sell. Check your insurance carefully, because standard personal policies often exclude commercial or rental use. Use the platform's protection plan, and take time-stamped condition photos before every rental.

Is renting a spare room passive income?

Not really. Short-term rentals like Airbnb can earn well, but cleaning, guest messages, check-ins and reviews make them closer to a part-time hospitality job.

Many cities now restrict or license short-term rentals, and most leases forbid subletting without permission. If you go ahead, check local rules first. Consider a longer-term lodger instead, which is less work per month but a bigger commitment. Count it as a side hustle rather than passive income. Does selling stuff you don't use count?

It's a quick win, but it's one-off income, not passive income. Once the item's sold, it stops earning.

That still makes it a smart first step. Selling things you'll never use again frees up cash and space, and the space itself might become a storage rental. Just don't mistake a good month of decluttering for a recurring income stream.

What should you check before renting out your stuff?

Check four things: permission, insurance, taxes and the platform's terms. Most rental headaches come from skipping one of them.

  • Permission: leases, HOA rules, mortgage terms and local laws can all restrict renting out space.
  • Insurance: personal policies often don't cover rental use, so ask your insurer directly.
  • Taxes: rental income is usually taxable. Keep records of what you earn and spend. This isn't tax advice, so check your local rules.
  • Platform terms: read the fees, the damage protection and how disputes are handled before you list.
Watch out: real rental platforms pay you. Anyone who asks you to pay a fee up front, or to cash a cheque and send money back, is running a scam. See passive income scams to avoid.

Which idea should you start with?

Start with whatever you own that's idle most of the time and needs the least handling. For most people, that's a parking spot, storage space or the laptop lid they already carry around.

Then layer ideas. Someone who works from a café three days a week, has an unused driveway and a garage corner could run all three with only an hour or two of setup between them. Questions people ask

What is the most passive way to earn from things you own?
Assets that need no handovers are the most passive: a parking spot with self-access, storage space with set access rules, or ad space on your laptop lid. Renting cars, gear or rooms earns but needs regular involvement.
Do I need insurance to rent out my stuff?
Usually you need to check it. Standard home and auto policies often exclude rental or commercial use, so ask your insurer and read the platform's protection terms before listing anything.
Is income from renting out my things taxable?
In most countries, yes. Keep records of what you earn and any related costs, and check your local tax rules or speak to a tax professional. This isn't tax advice.
Can renters rent out their parking spot or storage space?
Sometimes. Many leases and HOA rules forbid subletting space, so get written permission from your landlord or association first.
How much can I earn renting out things I own?
It varies widely with location, demand, season and the asset itself. Be wary of anyone promising a fixed amount, and start small to see what your area supports.
