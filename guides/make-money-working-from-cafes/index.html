Can working from a café actually make you money?

Yes, a little, if you treat café time as an asset rather than just a cost. You're already spending hours in a public place with a laptop open, and several parts of that routine can earn or save money.

Café working usually looks like a pure expense: coffee, snacks, maybe a train fare. But a regular café worker is also a familiar face in a busy space, carrying a screen-sized surface that faces a room full of people. Here's how to make that work in your favor.

How do you spend less while working from cafés?

The easiest money to make is money you don't spend. Think in terms of cost per working hour, not cost per drink.

  • Use loyalty programs. If you go to the same café or chain several times a week, a stamp card or app reward adds up quickly.
  • Pick a "home" café. Regulars often get better treatment: a friendly nod toward the outlet table, a refill, or a heads-up about the quiet hours.
  • Time your visits. Off-peak mornings and mid-afternoons mean less pressure to keep ordering and less need to leave when it fills up.
  • Split your day. Two shorter sessions in two places can work out cheaper, and feel less awkward, than one marathon at a single table.

What's good café working etiquette?

A simple rule of thumb is to buy something roughly every hour or two, and to give up a big table at peak times. Cafés that welcome laptop workers stay that way when laptop workers act like good customers.

  • Buy something when you arrive, and again if you stay through a busy period.
  • Don't spread over a four-person table when it's packed. Move to the bar or a two-top.
  • Take calls outside, or use headphones and keep your voice down.
  • Don't monopolize the only power outlet. Arrive charged.
  • Tip if the café has a tip jar and you're a regular.
Good manners help your sticker too. A Lidquid sticker sits on a lid in a café you already use. Being a regular the staff are happy to see keeps the arrangement easy for everyone.

Is a coworking day pass cheaper than a café?

Sometimes. It depends on how long you stay and how much you'd spend in a café over the same hours. A coworking day pass costs more up front but usually includes coffee, reliable Wi-Fi and no pressure to keep buying.

Prices vary a lot by city, so do the math with your own numbers:

café cost per day = drinks + food + transport · coworking cost per day = day pass + transport

For short sessions of two or three hours, a café usually wins. For a full eight-hour day with calls and focused work, a day pass can be both cheaper and more productive. Many people mix the two: coworking for heavy days, cafés for lighter ones. Coworking spaces are busy shared rooms too, so a laptop sticker earns there just as it does in a café.

Can you deduct café expenses from your taxes?

Possibly, if you're self-employed, but the rules are strict and depend on where you live. In many places, everyday coffee isn't deductible just because you worked while drinking it, while genuine business costs might be. Client meetings and workspace fees are examples.

This isn't tax advice. Tax rules differ by country and state and change over time. Keep receipts, note which work each expense supported, and check with a qualified accountant or your local tax authority before claiming anything.

Separately, any money you earn from side income, including laptop sticker advertising, may be taxable. Keep a simple record of what you earn.

How can café time help you find freelance work?

Being a regular somewhere is quiet, low-pressure networking. You'll see the same faces often: other freelancers, local business owners, and the café owner themselves, who might need a website, menu design, photos or social media help.

  • Be recognizable. Same café, similar times. People start conversations with familiar faces.
  • Have a one-line answer to "what do you do?" that makes it obvious how you could help.
  • Offer to help the café. Small local businesses often need freelance skills and like hiring regulars they already trust.
  • Let your laptop start conversations. A sticker that says "scan this" invites curiosity, and curiosity is the start of most conversations.
